## 0. Read this first

This playbook is written in a deliberate order, and the order is not the usual
marketing one. It leads with law and land title, not with positioning, because
in this specific category the legal facts decide *who you are even allowed to
sell to*. Two examples that make the point:

- **NRIs and OCIs cannot buy agricultural land, plantation property, or a
  farmhouse in India.** This is a FEMA restriction, not a paperwork hurdle you
  can work around. If your plan assumed NRIs were a core segment — a very
  common assumption in this category — that segment is closed to you for
  farm-classified inventory, and no amount of sales skill changes it.
- **In several states, a non-agriculturist cannot buy agricultural land at
  all,** or needs Collector permission. Maharashtra and Gujarat are restrictive;
  Karnataka liberalised in 2020; Telangana is open; Himachal is close to shut.
  So the same pitch deck is legal in one state and unsellable one state over.

Everything downstream — segments, pricing, channel, script — is a function of
those answers. That is why Section 1 comes first.

**This is a commercial playbook, not legal advice.** Land law in India is
state-specific, amended often, and enforced unevenly. Every legal point below is
flagged as something to confirm with a property lawyer licensed in your state.
Treat the legal sections as *the list of questions to take to counsel*, not as
clearance to proceed.

## 1. The legal gate — settle this before you spend a rupee on marketing

Six things determine whether you have a sellable product. Until they're
answered, any brochure you print risks being a liability document.

### 1.1 Land classification and who may buy

The core question: **is the land still classified agricultural, or has it been
converted to non-agricultural (NA) use?**

| State | Can a non-agriculturist buy agricultural land? | Notes to verify with counsel |
|---|---|---|
| Karnataka | Generally yes, post-2020 | The 2020 amendment to the Karnataka Land Reforms Act removed the old Sec. 79A/79B agriculturist-and-income restrictions |
| Maharashtra | Generally no, without permission | Sec. 63, Bombay Tenancy & Agricultural Lands Act, 1948; buyer typically must already be an agriculturist somewhere in India |
| Gujarat | Restrictive | Similar tenancy-act lineage to Maharashtra |
| Telangana / Andhra | Generally yes | Comparatively liberal; verify current land-records position |
| Tamil Nadu | Generally yes | Ceiling limits apply under the TN land-ceiling law |
| Himachal Pradesh | Effectively no | Sec. 118, HP Tenancy & Land Reforms Act — state permission needed for non-agriculturist non-Himachalis |
| Uttarakhand | Restrictive, and tightened recently | Confirm the current position — this has changed more than once |
| Kerala | Buying is possible; *conversion* is the constraint | Paddy land and wetland conversion is tightly controlled |

> This table is an orientation aid built from general practice, not a legal
> opinion, and several of these rules have been amended in the last few years.
> Confirm your state's current position before it appears in any sales material.

**If your land is agricultural and your state restricts non-agriculturist
purchase, you have three honest options:** convert to NA before selling, sell
only to buyers who already qualify as agriculturists, or obtain the permissions.
There is a fourth thing the market does — registering land in a qualifying
person's name for a non-qualifying buyer — and it exposes both sides to the
Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act. Do not build a sales motion on it.

### 1.2 Title and records

Non-negotiable, and the single most common reason these deals collapse late:

- Mother deed and 30-year title chain, with an advocate's title search report
- Revenue records — 7/12 extract (Maharashtra), RTC/pahani (Karnataka),
  khata/mutation, encumbrance certificate
- Survey sketch and demarcation matching what you will show on site
- No pending litigation, acquisition notification, tenancy claim, or bank charge
- Access: is the approach road on record, or is it someone's goodwill?

### 1.3 RERA

Plotted development typically requires RERA registration above a threshold
(commonly land over 500 sq m or more than eight units, with state variation).
Many "farm plot" schemes are marketed as outside RERA because the land is
agricultural. Confirm which side of that line you are on, in writing. Selling an
unregistered project that required registration is a penalty exposure and, in
practice, the first thing a sophisticated buyer's lawyer checks.

### 1.4 If you are selling "managed farmland" — read carefully

The model where buyers purchase a plot, you manage the plantation, and buyers
receive a share of produce or income is commercially attractive and legally
fraught. Where money is pooled, managed on investors' behalf, investors lack
day-to-day control, and the pitch is profit, the arrangement can fall under
**SEBI's Collective Investment Scheme regulations** and require registration.
This is not hypothetical — it is the regulatory response to the plantation-scheme
failures of the 1990s, and SEBI has acted against agro-land promoters since.

Practical consequence for your sales motion: **assured or projected returns are
the trigger.** "Buy this land, we can arrange management for a fee" sits in a
very different place from "invest ₹25 lakh, earn 12% a year from mango yield."
Get an opinion before any return figure appears in writing, in a deck, on a
website, or in a WhatsApp forward from your sales team.

### 1.5 What the buyer can actually build

Buyers pay a premium for the *idea* of a weekend home. Before you sell that idea:
what does the local development-control regulation permit on this classification
— permitted footprint, floor area, setbacks, whether a dwelling is allowed at
all on agricultural land, and whether "farmhouse" has a defined legal meaning in
your municipality? Also confirm sub-division legality: fragmenting agricultural
land into small plots is restricted in several states, and small-plot layouts
sold without proper sub-division approval are a recurring enforcement target.

### 1.6 Money handling

- Cash: Sec. 269ST caps cash receipts at ₹2 lakh, with penalty equal to the
  amount received; Sec. 269SS/269T restrict cash advances in property deals to
  ₹20,000. A cash component is normal in this market and is exactly the practice
  that ends a business. Bank everything.
- TDS: Sec. 194-IA applies 1% TDS on immovable property at ₹50 lakh and above —
  agricultural land is excluded, which is itself a signal of how the tax code
  treats your asset.
- Stamp duty and registration: state-specific, commonly 5–7% plus registration.
  Quote it to buyers up front; discovering it late kills momentum at closing.

**Checklist — do not launch marketing until every line is yes:**

- [ ] Land classification confirmed in writing
- [ ] Buyer-eligibility rule for your state confirmed by counsel
- [ ] Title search report clean, 30-year chain
- [ ] RERA position determined (registered, or documented as exempt)
- [ ] Buildability confirmed against local development-control rules
- [ ] Sub-division/layout approval in place if selling plots
- [ ] If managed-farmland: SEBI CIS opinion obtained
- [ ] Banking and receipt policy written down and briefed to the sales team

## 2. Who the buyer actually is

"Recent millionaires in India" is not one segment. It is at least five, with
different money, different triggers, and different objections.

### 2.1 Startup exit and ESOP liquidity
Founders post-secondary, and employees after an ESOP buyback or IPO. Concentrated
in Bengaluru, Gurugram/NCR, Mumbai, Pune, Hyderabad. Age 30–45. Suddenly liquid,
often for the first time, and consciously diversifying out of equity.

- **Buys because:** wants a hard asset after a paper-wealth decade; wants a place
  to disappear to; peer signalling within a tight founder network
- **Kills the deal:** title risk. This buyer will have a lawyer read everything
  and will walk on ambiguity rather than negotiate it
- **Reach via:** referral within the network, wealth managers, founder communities
- **Note:** the liquidity event is the trigger. Timing beats targeting here

### 2.2 SME promoters and traditional business owners
Tier-1 and tier-2 manufacturing, trading, distribution families. Often older
wealth than segment 2.1, and far more experienced at buying land.

- **Buys because:** land is the asset class they already trust; legacy and
  succession framing works; proximity to their existing operations matters
- **Kills the deal:** overpricing versus local circle rates and their own
  benchmarks. They know the market better than your sales team does
- **Reach via:** CA and family-office referral, trade bodies, direct relationship

### 2.3 Senior corporate and professional
CXO-level tech, banking, consulting; doctors and lawyers with practice income.
RSU-driven wealth, salaried cadence, spreadsheet-oriented.

- **Buys because:** lifestyle and retirement planning; a decade-out second home
- **Kills the deal:** total-cost-of-ownership uncertainty — maintenance, security,
  "who looks after it when I'm not there for eleven months"
- **Reach via:** LinkedIn targeting, employer-adjacent channels, wealth managers

### 2.4 New-wealth and creator economy
Trading and crypto gains, creators, sports and entertainment. Fast decisions,
high volatility, weaker documentation discipline.

- **Buys because:** status, content value of the property, speed
- **Kills the deal:** funding falls through; source-of-funds questions at closing
- **Handle with care:** enforce the same KYC and banking standards you apply
  everywhere. A fast close is not worth a compliance problem

### 2.5 NRIs and OCIs — excluded, and you must design for it

Under FEMA, NRIs and OCIs may acquire immovable property in India **other than
agricultural land, plantation property, and farmhouses**. Acquisition of those
categories is permitted essentially only by inheritance.

NRIs will nonetheless be among your most enthusiastic inbound leads, because the
emotional pull of "land back home" is strongest in exactly that group. You need a
standing, unambiguous answer, delivered early and in writing, rather than a
conversation that drifts toward a structure someone's cousin suggested. If part
of your inventory is NA-converted and does not meet the farmhouse or agricultural
definition, that portion may be a legitimate NRI offer — confirm with counsel and
then treat it as a *separate product line with separate collateral*, never as an
exception quietly made to the main one.

## 3. Positioning — pick one, not four

The default failure in this category is a brochure that promises a weekend
retreat, an organic-farming lifestyle, a 3x land-appreciation play, and a
tax-efficient structure, all at once. It reads as unserious to exactly the
analytical buyer you want.

Choose a primary:

**(a) Weekend home / second home.** Sells the drive, the air, the kids, the
Friday-evening escape. Strongest with 2.1 and 2.3. Requires you to be honest
about drive time in real traffic and about what is actually built today.

**(b) Land banking / long-hold.** Sells the corridor thesis — infrastructure,
an airport, an expressway, a plan document. Strongest with 2.2. Requires real
evidence, and it constrains what you may promise (see §7).

**(c) Wellness, organic, slow living.** Sells identity. Strongest with 2.1 and
2.4. Requires the operational substance to back it — someone has to actually
farm the land — and this is the positioning most likely to drift into a
managed-return pitch, so keep §1.4 in view.

**(d) Legacy and succession.** Sells permanence and inheritance. Strongest with
2.2 and older 2.3. The quietest pitch and often the highest conversion, because
it does not compete on IRR.

Secondary themes are fine in the body copy. One promise goes on the cover.

**On the tax angle:** agricultural income is exempt from income tax under
Sec. 10(1), and the market routinely pitches farmland as a tax play. Genuine
agricultural income from land the buyer actually farms is legitimately exempt.
Presenting the property *as a vehicle for exempting non-agricultural income* is
a different thing, and it creates liability for the buyer and for you. State the
tax position factually if asked; do not market on it. See §7.

## 4. Pricing and commercial structure

### 4.1 Anchor the price honestly
Establish, and be able to show: circle/guidance value, recent registered
transactions within a comparable radius, and per-unit pricing for genuinely
comparable inventory. Segment 2.2 will check all three. Being the party that
volunteers the comparison is worth more than the margin you would protect by
hiding it.

### 4.2 Structure the ladder
Price by plot size, road position, corner, view, and phase. Publish the ladder
internally so your team cannot improvise, and so an early buyer never discovers
that a later buyer paid less for a better plot — in a referral-driven segment
that single event can end a channel.

### 4.3 Payment milestones
Booking amount, agreement-to-sell, development milestones, registration. Keep
booking amounts refundable within a stated window: a refundable booking converts
far better with analytical buyers than a discount does, because it prices your
own confidence.

### 4.4 Your economics
Per unit, know: land cost or acquisition price, development cost, marketing cost
per closed sale, channel commission, statutory costs, and net margin. Then know
the same numbers per *lead*, because that is the number that decides whether a
channel lives or dies (§5).

## 5. Channels, ranked by expected return

In this segment, ranked roughly by what works — not by what is easiest to buy.

**1. Referral from existing buyers.** Highest conversion, lowest cost, and it
compounds. Requires you to have delivered honestly to the first cohort, which is
why §1 matters commercially and not just legally. Instrument it deliberately:
ask at handover, make introductions easy, and pay or recognise referrals openly.

**2. Wealth managers, CAs, and family offices.** The highest-leverage channel for
segment 2.1 and 2.2, because these advisors are consulted *at the liquidity
event* — the exact moment your product becomes relevant. Long to build, hard for
a competitor to displace. Approach with the title documentation, not the
brochure; this audience screens for risk before it screens for upside.

**3. Curated site experience.** The site visit is where farm property is actually
sold, so treat the visit as the product: transport, a hosted meal, an overnight
stay if you can, and a demarcated plot the buyer can stand on. Measure
visit-to-booking as your primary funnel metric.

**4. Founder and executive communities.** Alumni networks, founder groups,
industry forums. Works through participation, not advertising.

**5. Digital — intent and lookalike.** Google for high-intent search terms;
Meta lookalikes seeded from closed buyers, not from raw leads; LinkedIn for
segment 2.3 by seniority and employer. Expect high cost per qualified lead and
heavy junk volume. Qualify hard and early.

**6. Property portals.** Weak fit for premium farm inventory — the audience is
price-shopping apartments — but cheap to test. Judge on qualified leads, not
lead count.

**7. Events and luxury adjacency.** Golf, art, auto, wine. Expensive, slow,
occasionally produces one buyer who brings four more. Do it once you have a
closing motion that works, not before.

Test two channels at a time with a fixed budget and a fixed window, and kill on
cost-per-qualified-lead rather than on impressions.

## 6. Sales process

Expect a **3–9 month cycle**. Anyone promising you a two-week close in this
category is selling you something.

```
Lead → Qualified → Site visit → Second visit / family visit → EOI + booking
     → Agreement to sell → Due diligence → Registration → Handover → Referral
```

Stage definitions, so your pipeline means something:

| Stage | Exit criterion |
|---|---|
| Lead | Contactable, expressed interest |
| Qualified | Budget confirmed, eligibility confirmed (§1.1, §2.5), timeline stated |
| Site visit | Physically stood on a demarcated plot |
| Second visit | Returned, usually with spouse or family — the strongest single predictor of close |
| Booking | Booking amount received in bank |
| Agreement | Agreement to sell executed |
| Registration | Registered, stamp duty paid |

Two rules that matter more than the rest. **Qualify eligibility before the site
visit, not after** — a visit with a buyer who legally cannot purchase is pure
cost and, worse, tempts your team into creative structuring. And **give every
buyer the full document set before they ask.** In a category with this much
fraud, volunteering the title report is the strongest differentiator you have,
and it is nearly free.

## 7. What your team must never say

Write these into the sales brief and enforce them. Each one is a live legal or
reputational exposure, and each is said routinely in this market.

| Never say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "Assured returns of X%" | Nothing about returns without a SEBI CIS opinion (§1.4) |
| "Land will double in three years" | Historical transaction data, attributed, with a clear statement that past movement does not predict future |
| "You can show agricultural income and save tax" | The factual position: genuine agricultural income is exempt under Sec. 10(1); the buyer should consult their CA |
| "NRIs can buy through a structure" | NRIs and OCIs cannot acquire agricultural land, plantation property, or farmhouses under FEMA (§2.5) |
| "Registry in a farmer's name, you hold the plot" | Nothing — this is Benami exposure and must be refused outright |
| "Conversion is a formality, it'll come through" | The current status, the actual process, and the realistic timeline |
| "Part cash is fine" | Full banking channel, always (§1.6) |

Approve every claim in writing, and put a named person in charge of that
approval. WhatsApp messages from your sales team are discoverable and are, in
practice, where undertakings get made that nobody at the top ever authorised.

## 8. Objection handling

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "How do I know the title is clean?" | Hand over the advocate's title search report, 30-year chain, and EC. Invite their lawyer to inspect. Never ask them to take your word |
| "Can I even buy this — I'm not a farmer" | The state-specific answer, in writing, before the site visit |
| "What if it gets encroached?" | Fencing, demarcation, on-site security, caretaker arrangement, and the reporting cadence they will actually get |
| "Who maintains it? I'll visit twice a year" | The maintenance offer, the fee, what it covers, and the term. Say the annual number out loud |
| "How do I exit?" | Honest answer: farm land is illiquid, resale takes months to years. Show actual resale evidence if you have it. Refusing to concede illiquidity destroys credibility with segment 2.1 and 2.3 |
| "Can I build a house?" | The development-control answer from §1.5 — permitted footprint, approvals, timeline. Never "yes, everyone does it" |
| "It's cheaper 5 km away" | The specific difference: title, approvals, access road, infrastructure, phase. If there is no real difference, your price is wrong |
| "Is it RERA registered?" | The documented position either way (§1.3) |

## 9. Metrics

Track weekly. Six numbers, not a dashboard:

1. Qualified leads by channel
2. Cost per qualified lead by channel
3. Site visits booked, and site visits actually completed
4. **Visit-to-booking conversion** — the health metric for this business
5. Booking-to-registration conversion, and average days at each stage
6. Referrals generated per closed buyer

Benchmarks to set once you have 30 days of your own data — do not import
someone else's. Two leading indicators worth watching from day one: second-visit
rate, and the share of buyers who bring a lawyer (higher is better; it correlates
with buyers who close and do not later dispute).

## 10. First 90 days

**Days 1–30 — earn the right to sell.**
Close the §1 checklist. Commission the title search. Get the RERA determination
and, if relevant, the CIS opinion. Write the eligibility rule for your state into
a one-page team brief. Fix pricing against real comparables. Nothing external
ships this month.

**Days 31–60 — build the closing motion.**
Prepare the document pack (title report, approvals, layout, price ladder,
payment schedule). Design the site visit as a hosted experience. Write the sales
brief including §7. Open conversations with five wealth managers or CA firms.
Start one digital channel, small budget, purely to learn cost per qualified lead.
Target the first 10 site visits.

**Days 61–90 — sell, and instrument it.**
Run 30+ site visits. Close the first cohort. Debrief every loss by reason and
fix the top one. Ask every buyer for a referral at handover. Kill the channel
with the worst cost per qualified lead and double the best one.

## 11. Anti-patterns

Each of these is common in this market, and each is how operators in this
category end up in litigation or in the news.

- **Selling before the title is clean**, intending to fix it during the cycle
- **Promising returns** on anything, in any medium, ever (§1.4)
- **Accepting cash** because the buyer prefers it (§1.6)
- **Structuring around buyer ineligibility** rather than declining the sale
- **Selling sub-divided agricultural plots** without lawful sub-division
- **Discounting inconsistently** — fatal in a referral-driven segment (§4.2)
- **Optimising for lead volume** instead of qualified leads and visits
- **Letting sales improvise claims** on WhatsApp with no approval trail
